## 1. The Sabu Disk — 5,000 Years of the Same Impulse

On 19 January 1936, British archaeologist Walter Bryan Emery opened Mastaba S3111 at the Saqqara necropolis in Egypt. Inside Room E — the central burial chamber — he found not the remains of Prince Sabu at the focal point, but a stone disk. The artifact, carved from weakly metamorphic siltstone, measures 61 centimetres in diameter and 10.6 centimetres in height. Three wings or lobes fold inward toward a central hole of approximately 8 centimetres, creating a form that, when viewed from above, resembles a steering wheel with three very wide spokes.

The Sabu Disk is unique in Egyptology. While stone vessel production peaked during the First to Third Dynasties (c. 3000-2600 BCE), and several high-quality slate objects have been recovered from Saqqara, no other artifact shares this design. Theories about its purpose range from impeller or water pump to oil lamp to ritual object. Airbus Research Centre produced 3D-printed copies and confirmed the form has aerodynamic properties, though its rotational symmetry (non-chirality) makes use as a propeller or turbine impossible.

What matters for iAAi is not what the disk did, but what it represents: the oldest known artifact that embodies the concept of rotational integration — multiple lobes converging on a single axis. The disk was placed at the centre of the tomb, not the body. The equation was more important than the person. Infrastructure engineers have always understood this.
